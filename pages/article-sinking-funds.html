Sinking Funds: What They Are, How They Work, and How to Set Them Up

A sinking fund turns a large, predictable expense into a series of smaller savings decisions. Instead of waiting for an annual bill, holiday, repair, or other known cost to hit your budget, you build the money before you need it.

Published by The Wealth Sage · About 11 min read

Most budgets look fine when every expense arrives on schedule. The problem appears when several large costs land in the same month: an insurance renewal, car maintenance, holiday spending, a school expense, or a planned trip.

Those expenses may feel like emergencies because they arrive irregularly. But an expense does not become an emergency simply because it is large. A sinking fund gives predictable costs their own place in the system.

The goal is simple: make irregular expenses regular savings decisions.

What Is a Sinking Fund?

A sinking fund is money you set aside gradually for a specific future expense that is expected or reasonably foreseeable but does not occur every month.

Instead of trying to find $1,200 when a bill arrives, you might set aside $100 a month for twelve months. The expense has not become cheaper; the cash-flow problem has been spread across time.

This is consistent with how major financial education sources describe the strategy. MoneyHelper describes sinking funds as regular savings for known upcoming costs, while NerdWallet describes them as dedicated savings built over time for predictable expenses such as travel, holidays, home repairs, or renewals. MoneyHelper's guide and NerdWallet's guide both distinguish the strategy from emergency savings.

Sinking Fund vs. Emergency Fund

This is the most important distinction to get right.

Sinking Fund Emergency Fund
For an expected or foreseeable expenseFor a genuine unexpected financial shock
Usually tied to a purpose or deadlineHeld as a broader financial buffer
Used when the planned expense arrivesUsed when something necessary and unexpected disrupts normal cash flow
Examples: annual insurance, gifts, travel, planned repairsExamples: sudden job loss, urgent medical cost, major unexpected repair

MoneyHelper makes the distinction directly: if you know a cost is coming, it belongs in a sinking fund; an emergency fund is for unexpected costs. Military OneSource similarly describes sinking funds as money set aside little by little for specific upcoming costs, while emergency savings is intended for unexpected costs. MoneyHelper and Military OneSource are useful references for the distinction.

Why Sinking Funds Matter

A sinking fund solves a timing problem.

Your annual insurance bill may be affordable in total but difficult to absorb from one month's income. The same is true of holiday spending, tuition, property-related costs, car maintenance, or replacing an aging appliance.

Saving gradually changes the shape of the decision. Instead of asking, “How will I pay for this when it arrives?” you ask, “How much needs to be set aside before it arrives?”

That shift can also reduce the temptation to place predictable costs on a credit card or use an emergency reserve for something that was known in advance. NerdWallet's 2026 coverage makes the same practical point: sinking funds can help people cover major predictable expenses without relying on credit cards or draining emergency savings. NerdWallet's January 2026 report includes examples and survey methodology.

Common Sinking Fund Categories

Not every irregular expense needs its own account. The best categories are usually expenses that are large enough to disrupt the month, predictable enough to plan for, and important enough that you do not want to borrow to cover them.

  • Car costs: insurance, registration, maintenance, tires, or a planned replacement.
  • Home costs: repairs, maintenance, furniture replacement, or other planned projects.
  • Annual bills: insurance premiums, memberships, software, or renewals that do not arrive monthly.
  • Holidays and gifts: birthdays, holiday shopping, weddings, and other recurring celebrations.
  • Travel: a planned vacation or other known trip.
  • Education: school supplies, tuition-related costs, uniforms, or activities.
  • Health: predictable dental, optical, deductible, or treatment costs where applicable.
  • Pets: routine annual veterinary costs or other foreseeable expenses.
  • Taxes: expected tax payments where income or local rules make a separate reserve useful.

Recent guidance from MoneyHelper and budgeting platforms such as Aspire Budgeting lists many of the same categories, including car expenses, home maintenance, gifts, holidays, school costs, medical expenses, and pet care. MoneyHelper and Aspire's setup guide.

How to Calculate a Sinking Fund

The basic calculation is simple:

Monthly contribution = (target amount − current balance) ÷ months until the expense

For example, suppose you expect a $1,200 expense in ten months and have nothing saved yet:

$1,200 ÷ 10 = $120 per month.

If you already have $300 saved, the remaining amount is $900:

($1,200 − $300) ÷ 10 = $90 per month.

The formula becomes more useful when you use real numbers from previous bills instead of guessing. MoneyHelper recommends reviewing past statements when estimating future costs and notes that a modest cushion can help account for price increases. MoneyHelper.

How to Set Up Sinking Funds Step by Step

1. Look Back Before You Look Forward

Review the last twelve months of spending. Look for expenses that were large, irregular, and foreseeable.

Search your bank and card statements for insurance, repairs, gifts, travel, subscriptions, school costs, medical bills, and annual renewals.

2. Give Each Important Expense a Target

Write down the expected amount and the date you expect to need it. The goal is not perfect forecasting. It is to turn vague future spending into a visible target.

3. Calculate the Monthly Amount

Use the remaining cost and the number of months available. If the deadline is close, the required monthly contribution will be higher. If you start earlier, each contribution can be smaller.

4. Choose the Simplest Storage System

You do not necessarily need a separate bank account for every category. A bank with labeled savings buckets can work, as can a simple spreadsheet or budgeting system.

NerdWallet and MoneyHelper both emphasize keeping the purpose of the money clear. The implementation can vary; the important part is knowing what each amount is for and not confusing it with everyday spending. NerdWallet.

5. Automate the Contribution

Move the planned amount shortly after income arrives instead of waiting until the end of the month.

This is where sinking funds connect naturally with Pay Yourself First: both systems make an important decision earlier, before flexible spending can absorb the money.

6. Use the Fund for Its Intended Job

When the expense arrives, use the money for the expense. Then decide whether the category should be closed, rolled forward, or rebuilt for the next cycle.

How Many Sinking Funds Should You Have?

There is no universal number. Too few categories can make the system vague; too many can turn a simple strategy into another administrative task.

A practical starting point is to choose your three to five highest-impact categories. Start with expenses that are expensive, predictable, and capable of damaging your monthly cash flow if you ignore them.

MoneyHelper specifically warns that too many sinking funds can become complicated and suggests keeping the number manageable, while NerdWallet recommends starting with a few high-priority funds and adding more only when the system remains easy to manage. MoneyHelper and NerdWallet.

Do Sinking Funds Need Separate Bank Accounts?

No.

A sinking fund is primarily a financial system or category, not a particular type of bank account. You can hold several goals inside one savings account using labeled buckets, or use multiple accounts when separation makes your system easier to manage.

The right choice depends on what reduces mistakes for you. If seeing all of the money in one balance makes you accidentally spend money reserved for another purpose, more separation may help. If ten accounts make your system impossible to maintain, simplify.

Where Should You Keep Sinking Fund Money?

For many short-term sinking funds, the priority is accessibility and stability rather than investment growth.

A savings account may fit that job. A high-yield savings account can also be considered when the account terms, access, fees, and deposit-insurance arrangements fit your needs. Read What Is a High-Yield Savings Account? for the details.

Money you expect to need soon should not automatically be exposed to the same market risk as money intended for long-term wealth building. The time horizon should determine the job.

Sinking Funds and the 50/30/20 Rule

The 50/30/20 framework gives you broad buckets. Sinking funds make part of the saving bucket more specific.

For example, instead of treating all savings as one vague number, you might identify:

  • Emergency reserve
  • Car costs
  • Annual bills
  • Travel
  • Gifts and holidays

The point is not to force a particular percentage. It is to make future obligations visible enough that the budget can prepare for them.

See The 50/30/20 Rule for the broader budgeting framework.

Sinking Funds and Lifestyle Creep

Sinking funds can also expose spending that looks harmless when viewed one purchase at a time.

A $900 annual subscription, a $1,500 holiday budget, or a series of upgrades may not look large as individual transactions. Once converted into a monthly sinking-fund requirement, the recurring cost becomes easier to see.

This creates a useful question:

If I cannot comfortably fund the future version of this expense, can I comfortably afford the expense today?

That is one reason sinking funds work well beside the Lifestyle Creep framework.

What About Unexpected Car Repairs?

This is one of the gray areas.

Routine maintenance, tires, registration, and a known replacement date are predictable enough for a sinking fund. A completely unexpected major breakdown may belong in an emergency fund.

You can also use both systems together: a car sinking fund for predictable ownership costs and an emergency reserve for genuinely unexpected shocks.

Common Sinking Fund Mistakes

Creating too many funds

If every small expense becomes its own category, the system can become harder to maintain than the problem it was designed to solve.

Starting too late

A sinking fund works best when you begin before the deadline creates pressure. Starting ten or twelve months earlier can radically reduce the required monthly contribution compared with starting two months before the bill.

Guessing instead of checking past spending

Use previous statements, receipts, renewal notices, or actual quotes when possible. A realistic target is better than a convenient one.

Using sinking funds as an excuse to spend more

The system does not make every planned expense affordable. It simply makes the cost visible earlier.

Raiding the fund for unrelated spending

If money has a purpose, moving it to cover a different want destroys the information the system was giving you.

A Simple Monthly Sinking Fund System

You can run the entire system in a short monthly review:

  1. List the next twelve months of large or irregular expenses.
  2. Estimate the target amount for each.
  3. Subtract what is already saved.
  4. Divide each remainder by the months until it is needed.
  5. Automate the total contribution.
  6. Review the categories once a month.
  7. After an expense, decide whether to close, roll over, or rebuild the fund.

The purpose is not to create a perfect spreadsheet. It is to make future expenses less capable of surprising your present-day budget. These categories can also sit inside a zero-based budget when you want every dollar assigned a job.

Plan the expense. Fund the expense. Spend the fund when the expense arrives.

The Bigger Financial System

Sinking funds are one layer, not the entire financial plan.

Budgeting helps you see where current income goes. Pay Yourself First protects important priorities before flexible spending. An emergency fund protects against genuine shocks. And once the cash foundation is working, surplus can eventually become assets and support long-term wealth building.

Income → Budget → Sinking Funds → Emergency Buffer → Surplus → Assets → Wealth
